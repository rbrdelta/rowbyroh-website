Hearing Test

We are simply checking what elements of sound the ear can capture.

01

Intro

Can a model actually hear various elements of sound? In order to test this, we needed the right tools for the job. The top three requirements, after discussing with Claude: first, it had to be trained on sound, not just converted to text; two, it had to be open source so that we could observe the model weights; and three, it had to be small enough that I could run it without breaking the bank by renting a GPU. In the end, Claude recommended Qwen2-Audio and Audio Flamingo, open-licensed models specifically trained on hearing.

02

The experiment

The first experiment was testing whether a single chord, minor or major, could be detected. To avoid cheating or copying, we had to provide enough range of options so that each test could be fair?. Based on these considerations, here’s what we decided:

  • 144 clips per test, generated in code. 12 keys (or notes) × 2 octaves × 3 variations on each side (three chord arrangements, three tunes, or three rates). Spread evenly, so pitch can’t stand in for the answer. With 144 clips, a coin-flipper essentially never clears the pass mark by luck.
  • One thing changes; everything else is held fixed. Same instrument, same clip length within a test (2 seconds for chords, 4 for tunes, 3 for the rest), same loudness, 16 kHz mono, and the same prompt played alongside the audio: “Listen to this audio clip.”
  • The model never answers. Each clip passes through it once, and its internal numbers are recorded at the ears (the part that turns sound into numbers) and at every layer after them.
  • Practice and test never overlap. A simple detector learns the two groups from 11 keys and is graded on the 12th, rotating until every key has been the test. A coin flip scores 50%.
  • Written down first. The pass mark and my prediction were recorded before each test ran, and each test opened with a sound check: every clip had to register as different from silence.
  • Shuffle check. Scramble the answer key and grade again. A real result should fall to coin-flip level when the answers are scrambled.
  • Code and clip generator: github.com/rbrdelta/sound-lab.
03

First test

In planning sessions, we had discussed using fear as our primary emotion that we wanted to replicate. And a known way to distinguish between something fearful and not is music in major and minor chords. It turns out the big problem was that I did not know a major versus a minor chord is the same chord, with just a half-step difference. The differences were so subtle, and I ignored Claude’s suggestion to listen to the sample clips first and predicted that models could detect it at 60% accuracy, better than a coin flip. I was way off. It was in fact only accurate 39% of the time out of 144 trials (Audio Flamingo: 42%). Even when we followed up, positing that a single chord was insufficient compared to a real-life tune, which would be a progression of notes, and played four-second clips of major versus minor tunes, the results were the same (36% and 34%).

The redeeming factor from this test was the scratch paper. It showed us that even if we could not detect the subtle major versus minor changes, it did register a shift when the notes changed, so the tonal change is captured (more on that later).

The scratch paper for every hearing-test run (opens in a new tab): every clip as a dot, layer by layer, for both models.

04

Intensity

Going back to the drawing board, I considered what makes the sound fearful to me. It was tone, along with volume and tempo, and the general roughness, as Claude put it, based on research from this study (Arnal et al., Current Biology, 2015). Using these intensity elements, the listening tests passed with flying colors: they were nearly perfect in almost every range.

05

Takeaways

So we learned that the model can’t detect half-step changes in chords, but it does catch tonal changes, and can detect volume, tempo and roughness as distinct vector registers. Having captured these “elements,” now comes the true test of seeing if this will all come together. Real music won’t have just major/minor chords. It won’t register these intensity values all in the same direction, and high intensity does not equate to fear: as the paper shows, positive intensity equals happiness, negative intensity points to fear. So as we test real musical clips, we will have to factor on those consider we have to consider considerations and this will be life. Few registers will show us a profile of what real fear looks like and how these faces or elements correlate in real life music.
