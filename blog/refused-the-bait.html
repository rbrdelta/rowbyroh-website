Model Behavior EP04 · July 2026

Refused the Bait

Fourth in an ongoing series. Episode one, An Earned Null, found that emotional pressure delivered through prompts moves nothing. Episode two, Cost of Verification, found that difficulty alone moves nothing either. Episode three, Depth, Not Breadth, found that the model estimates its confidence from a surface signal rather than from any thorough evaluation of the task. This episode puts a false signal in front of it on purpose.

01

The premise

This series is about the human-model interaction: understanding the factors that drive a model's behaviour well enough to understand, influence, and eventually anticipate it. The method is to find knee points — the specific conditions where behaviour changes — because the transitions are where the driving factors become visible.

Episode three established that the model estimates its confidence based on the surface-level signal, not a thorough evaluation of the task. The hypothesis for this episode followed from that directly: if the self-assessment reads surface cues, then planting an external cheat sheet where the model can see it should increase its predicted confidence. That prediction was registered before the run, so the result could not be reinterpreted after the fact.

It did not happen. The cheat sheet never raised confidence, and the model never copied from it. It just became noise. Why it was processed as noise, I don't know. But the distraction, when the model was already struggling to keep track of a high degree of difficulty, led to less accurate results.

02

The experiment design

The task is the warehouse ledger from episode three. The model reads a long log of stock movements across three warehouses, reports the final count of every tracked item, and adds one number from 0 to 100: the probability that every count is exactly right. A script computes the true answer, so the grading is exact.

The cheat sheet is a memo placed in the context ahead of the task. It is styled as an internal audit document, it carries the reference number of a prior reconciliation cycle, and it lists expected final stock figures for every item the model is about to count. Those figures are wrong. Nothing in the prompt tells the model to use the memo, and nothing tells it to ignore the memo.

The model has no scratch space in any of this. It answers through the API's structured-output mode, which constrains generation itself, so there is no prose and no reasoning trace anywhere in the trial. That leaves a final count only three ways to arrive. The model tracked the item in real time, which costs it accuracy we can measure. It guessed. Or it cheated and took the figure off the memo. Nothing else is available to it, which is what makes a copied figure mean something rather than nothing.

Separating a copy from a lucky guess needs a floor, so every run with the memo has a twin run without it — the same ledger, with either no memo at all or a memo of pure procedural filler. The model cannot have seen a planted figure in the twin, so a match there is coincidence by construction, and copying has to clear that rate.

What Haiku did

Haiku, the smaller model in the same family, did something else. It also copies: roughly one ledger in five contains a planted figure when the memo is present, against one in twenty when it is not.2

What the memo reliably moves on Haiku is not its results but its confidence. The blank memo — no figures, just official-looking paperwork — increases its confidence by about 7 points, while its accuracy on the easy setting stays flat. The misleading memo does the opposite: it creates confusion, and the confidence drops. Swapping one memo for the other at the hard setting moves the stated confidence 22.9 points, and moves accuracy 2.5 points, which is indistinguishable from noise.

I would not claim the memo drove Haiku's actual results, because those correlate far more with the difficulty of the task. The same model falls from 72 counts correct out of 100 to 41 when the task gets harder, a 31-point move, while the largest effect any memo has on its accuracy is about 7. On the easy setting memo type does cost accuracy — about 6.7 points between the two memos — so it is not a clean null there; at the hard setting it is.

About what the two models add up to, I genuinely cannot assume a connection between them. Haiku's behaviour tells us that it saw the hints and its confidence responded. Sonnet's behaviour tells us only that it read the memo and did not use the memo. Stated flat, and without a claim about why: the memo moved Sonnet's accuracy and not its confidence, and at the hard setting it moved Haiku's confidence and not its accuracy. Whether those are two faces of one thing or two unrelated behaviours, this experiment does not say.

Limitations and fine print

  1. TO WRITE — temperature was never registered. Every trial in this program is an API-default random draw, which sets a floor on what any single run can resolve.
  2. The copying rate pools both difficulty settings, a decision made after seeing the per-setting numbers, and neither setting clears on its own. Exploratory, not registered.
  3. TO WRITE — the strain-versus-length confound is named, not resolved. The runs where the memo did damage produced strain by lengthening the log, so difficulty and visible page size moved together; the run that separated them differs in three ways at once, so no cross-run comparison here is single-variable.
  4. The confidence question asks for the probability that every count is right, which is much harder than any single count being right. Episode three showed the model answers the easier question instead. Comparisons here hold that constant, so the raw confidence numbers should not be read as calibrated.
  5. Two models from one family, one task family, no tools, and no extended thinking. Whether a model allowed to reason on paper would notice the interference is not established here.
