Second in the series. Episode 0 sets out the premise and the five steps; this episode covers steps 1 and 2.

01

The question for this step

Step 2 asks whether a model can listen accurately: when the sound changes, do the model’s internal numbers change with it? It is not yet a test of whether the model detects fear. That is step 3.

We are simply checking what elements of sound the ear can capture.

02

Picking the model

I was open to exploring many models. We had discussed audio models like Gemini as well, but I thought it best to go straight to the source, to see if we can push the boundaries and see if any model can really "hear" music.

Two constraints narrowed the field. Claude, the model the Model Behavior track studied, takes no audio: its voice mode turns speech into text with a separate system, so the model never hears sound. And reading a model’s internal numbers requires a model whose weights are open. Claude proposed two open models that take audio directly: Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct (Apache-2.0 license), tested first, and NVIDIA’s Audio Flamingo Next, trained on more music, as the fallback (its license allows non-commercial, academic use). Both models’ “ears” (the audio encoder that turns sound into numbers) descend from Whisper, a speech-recognition system, so hearing music was a real question, not a formality. In the end, both models ran every test.

03

Which sounds might carry fear

The cue list is just my idea of what constitutes a fearful sound, along with tone. There may be other elements that play a stronger or weaker part, but I don't know that yet. I see this as iterative: if we can hear these cues, and play music that matches these cues, and it doesn't flag fear, then we go back to the drawing board and re-examine the elements of fear. But I'd rather start with a premise than not start at all. I'm OK with being wrong, haha.

Four cues, one test each:

  • Major vs minor. The same chord or tune, with one or two notes a half-step lower in the minor version.
  • Roughness. One held note whose loudness flutters either slowly (3–6 times a second) or fast (40–100 times a second). Research on human screams found that screams carry this fast flutter, in a range ordinary speech doesn’t use verify: Arnal et al., Current Biology, 2015.
  • Volume. The same eight loudness levels, visited either gradually, gliding in order, or in jumps with instant cuts. The first design changed both the range of loudness and how abruptly it changed; it was revised so the range stayed fixed and only the abruptness moved.
  • Tempo. The same short note repeated at a resting-heartbeat pace (60–80 a minute) or a racing one (160–200 a minute).
04

How every test worked

Each test used 144 clips generated in code. Everything was held fixed except the one cue being tested: the instrument, the clip length, the loudness, and the prompt played alongside the audio (“Listen to this audio clip.”). The clips were spread evenly across 12 notes or keys and two octaves, so pitch could not stand in for the answer.

The model never answered anything. Each clip passed through it once, and its internal numbers were recorded at the ears and at every layer after them. A simple detector then tried to sort the clips into the two groups: average one group’s numbers, average the other’s, draw a line between them. It was graded only on keys or notes it had never practised on. A coin flip scores 50%. The pass mark and the prediction for each test were written down before it ran, and every test began with a sound check: every clip had to register as different from silence. The code and clip generator are public at github.com/rbrdelta/sound-lab.

06

What happened

Both models separated all three physical cues, roughness, volume and tempo, at 99–100% on notes they had never practised on. Both failed major vs minor, scoring below a coin flip on held chords and on short tunes.

After hearing the major/minor clips, my prediction would have been different, but that was a lesson in actually checking the samples first. Major vs minor was a good theory, but too subtle.

07

What it heard, and what it didn’t

Read with the same simple detector, the models’ ears appear to register which notes change and how high they are, but carry no shared sense of major vs minor. Within a single key, the internal numbers do shift when the middle note drops a half-step, but the shift points a different way in every key. A detector trained on 11 keys therefore points backwards on the 12th, which is why the scores came in below a coin flip rather than at it. This pattern appeared in all four major/minor runs, on both models, but it was not predicted in advance, so it is a lead rather than a finding. Relationships between notes beyond major vs minor were not tested.

We know it hears the note, so that's why we're proceeding to checking with music.

08

Did we measure the right thing?

For roughness, volume and tempo: did we measure the right thing?

As a check, the answer key was shuffled at random while the model’s readings stayed untouched. With shuffled labels, the detector fell to 49–52%, coin-flip level. With the real labels, it scored 99–100%. The detector succeeds only when the two groups reflect a real difference in what the model hears. The groups are named for what they literally are, fast vs slow flutter, jumping vs gradual loudness, fast vs relaxed tempo, rather than “fear” and “calm”: nothing in these tests knows about fear.

09

What this doesn’t show

  • Hearing a cue is not feeling fear. That is step 3.
  • Every cue that passed is a high-intensity cue. Each fits excitement as well as fear, so the next step separates positive vs negative feeling from intensity.
  • The two groups in each passing test were far apart by design. Where the line falls between them was not tested.
  • Faster tempo also meant more notes and more total sound, so the tempo result cannot say which of those the model used.
  • Jumping loudness adds tiny clicks at each cut. They are part of what “abrupt” sounds like, but they are a separate cue.
10

Calibration

This is all a calibration exercise for me, because I don't know how accurate these models are. I'm building a register of what an LLM can do as well. So it'll fluctuate, and it's a learning process.

11

Up next

The set includes high-intensity music that is not negative, such as triumphant cues, so a detector that only reads intensity gets caught.
