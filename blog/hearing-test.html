Hearing Test

[Subtitle — Daniel]

Second in the series. Episode 0 sets out the premise and the five steps; this is step 2.

01

The question for this step

"2. Once we posit these factors, can we find a model that precisely 'listen' to these sounds?"

Hearing Test Debrief (Notion), step 2

"we are simply checking what elements of sound the ear can capture."

Session, 2026-10-06

Facts: this step checks whether the model listens accurately (its internal numbers change when the sound changes). It does not check whether it detects fear; that is step 3.

02

Picking the model

"I was open to exploring many models - we had discussed just audio models like Gemini as well, but I thought it best to go straight to the source to see if we can push the boundaries and see if any model can really 'hear' music. that's why you proposed these two."

Session, 2026-10-06

Facts (attribute plainly: Claude proposed the two candidates): Claude takes no audio at all; voice mode is transcription by a separate system. Reading a model's internal numbers needs open weights. Candidates: Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct (Apache-2.0 license), first; Audio Flamingo Next (NVIDIA, trained on more music; non-commercial "academic purposes only" license), fallback. Both have ears derived from Whisper, a speech recognizer, which is why hearing music was a real question and not a formality.

03

Which sounds might carry fear

"the cue list is just my idea of what constitutes a fearful sound along with tone. there may be other elements that play a stronger or weaker part, but I don't know that yet. I see this as iterative. IF we can hear these cues and play music that match these cues and it doesn't flag fear, then we go back to the drawing board and we re-examine the elements of fear. but I'd rather start with a premise then to not start at all. I'm ok with being wrong, haha."

Session, 2026-10-06

"it's not about the pitch, it's about the intonations"

Roughness prediction, 2026-10-06

Facts: four cues, one test each: major vs minor; roughness (loudness fluttering fast, 30–150 times a second, the range research on human screams calls "rough" — verify citation before publishing: Arnal et al., Current Biology, 2015); volume (gradual vs jumping); tempo (relaxed vs fast). The volume fix (range held fixed so only abruptness moved) followed from that same scream research.

04

How every test worked

Facts, from the registration file:

  • 144 clips per test, made in code. Everything held fixed except the one thing being tested: instrument, length, loudness, the prompt ("Listen to this audio clip.").
  • Spread evenly across 12 notes or keys and 2 octaves, so pitch can't stand in for the answer.
  • The model never answers. Each clip goes through once and the internal numbers are read at the ears (the audio encoder) and at every layer after.
  • The detector: average one group's numbers, average the other's, draw the line between. Graded only on keys or notes it never practised on.
  • A coin flip scores 50%. Pass marks and Daniel's prediction were written down before each test ran.
  • Sound check first, every test: every clip registers as different from silence.

06

What happened

Major vs minor "was a good theory but too subtle."

Sessions, 2026-10-06

07

What it heard, and what it didn't

"we know it hears the note. so that's why we're proceeding to checking with music."

Session, 2026-10-06

Agreed reading: the models hear which notes change and how high they are, but have no shared sense of major vs minor; relationships between notes beyond that weren't tested. Within one key, the numbers do change when the middle note moves, but the change points a different way in every key, so a detector trained on 11 keys points backwards on the 12th — why the scores came in below a coin flip. Seen in all four runs on both models; not tested in advance, so it's a lead, not a finding. Both models hear physical changes over time (flutter, loudness, speed) almost perfectly.

Want to go deeper? The scratch paper for every hearing-test run: every clip as a dot, layer by layer, for both models.

08

Did we measure the right thing?

"For roughness, volume and tempo did we measure the right thing? Do a sanity check."

Hearing Test Debrief (Notion)

Facts: with the answer key shuffled at random (readings untouched), the detector falls to 49–52%; with the real key, 99–100%. Groups named for what they are (fast vs slow flutter, jumping vs gradual loudness, fast vs relaxed tempo), not "fear" and "calm": nothing in these tests knows about fear.

09

What this doesn't show

  • Hearing a cue is not feeling fear. That's step 3.
  • Every cue that passed is high-intensity: it fits excitement as well as fear. Next step separates positive vs negative from intensity — "positivity/negativity or pessimism/optimism or hope/despair."
  • The passing groups were far apart by design; where the line sits in between is untested.
  • Tempo also changed how many notes played and the average loudness, so that pass can't say which one the ears used.
  • Jumping loudness adds tiny clicks at each cut — part of what "abrupt" sounds like, but separable.
